# Chapter 9: When the Author Is Not Human

*This chapter is an argument. The evidence it rests on is verified and attributed; the
reasoning built on that evidence is mine, and I have tried to make it falsifiable rather
than merely persuasive. Checked August 2026.*

Everything before this chapter has been teaching. This one is advocacy, and it would be
dishonest to let you discover that gradually. So: I am about to argue for a position, that
position happens to be the one this project is built on, and you should read it with the
suspicion that deserves.

I will try to earn it by being precise about which parts are measured and which are mine,
and by ending with the conditions under which I would be wrong.

## The assumption nobody wrote down

Volume I contains a load-bearing assumption that appears nowhere in its text, because
until recently it was too obvious to state.

**A human being writes the configuration, and a human being reads what the tools say
about it.**

Look at how completely the safety machinery depends on that. A linter finds a problem and
*reports* it to someone, who reads the report, understands it, and decides. A code review
catches a mistake because a person reads a diff. Volume I's Chapter 14 spent its length
arguing that diagnostics should name the consequence rather than the rule, should point at
the line you wrote, should explain the mechanism: every one of those is a claim about what
serves a *reader*. The chapter's own example diagnostic is typeset for human eyes, with
carets under the offending span.

Detection, as a strategy, is an argument addressed to somebody. Its value is not in finding
the problem; it is in the finding *reaching a mind that acts*.

That assumption is now partially false, and the interesting question is not whether
machine-written configuration is good or bad. It is what happens to a safety strategy whose
foundation was an assumption about reading.

## What has actually been measured

Before the argument, the evidence, because this is the part I did not make up, and it is
stronger than I expected when I went looking.

The DORA research programme surveyed delivery performance across thousands of
organisations and reported in 2025 that *"90% of survey respondents report using AI at
work"* and that *"more than 80% believe it has increased their productivity."* In the same
report: *"30% report little or no trust in the code generated by AI."*

Then the two delivery findings, which need to be held together. First: *"Unlike last year,
we observe a positive relationship between AI adoption on both software delivery throughput
and product performance."* Second: *"However, AI adoption does continue to have a negative
relationship with software delivery stability."*

Volume I's Chapter 2 built an entire argument on DORA's older, central finding: that speed
and stability rise *together*, because the practices producing one produce the other. That
finding is why "deploy more often to be more stable" is not a paradox. So a practice that
raises throughput while lowering stability is, against that programme's own historical
result, genuinely anomalous and demands an explanation rather than a shrug.

The report offers one, and it is mechanical: *"AI accelerates software development, but that
acceleration can expose weaknesses downstream. Without robust control systems, like strong
automated testing, mature version control practices, and fast feedback loops, an increase
in change volume leads to instability."*

And the framing the report leads with: *"AI doesn't fix a team; it amplifies what's already
there."*

One more observation, which is analysis of the report rather than a headline finding of it:
the time AI saves during creation is frequently reallocated to auditing and verifying what
was produced. AI **shifts** the work required for delivery rather than removing it.

That is the whole factual basis. Everything after this point is me.

## The argument

**Premise one: generation got dramatically cheaper, and review did not.**

Writing a hundred lines of Kubernetes configuration used to cost a person an hour. It now
costs a prompt. Reviewing a hundred lines of Kubernetes configuration well (actually
checking that the selector matches the labels, that the budget leaves eviction room, that
the probe is not pointed at a dependency) costs a person about what it always did, because
it is bounded by human reading and human judgement.

Those two costs used to be roughly balanced. They are not any more, and nothing on the
horizon rebalances them, because the cost of review is bounded by the reviewer.

**Premise two: when one stage of a pipeline gets much faster, the bottleneck moves to the
next one.**

This is not a claim about AI at all; it is an old claim about pipelines, and it is why
Volume I's Chapter 2 cared so much about where the slow step was. Speed up one stage and
the constraint relocates to the next.

What DORA measured (throughput up, stability down) is what that relocation looks like
from the outside. The reallocation-to-auditing observation, which is analysis rather than a
headline finding, is what it looks like from the inside. The work did not vanish. It moved
to the step that cannot absorb it.

**Premise three: detection assumes a reader, and therefore degrades exactly when reading is
the bottleneck.**

Here is the pivot, and it is the part I most want you to test.

A checker that *detects* a problem produces a diagnostic. That diagnostic has value only if
someone reads it and acts. When the volume of generated change was matched to human reading
capacity, this was fine: the diagnostic arrived in a review a person was already doing.

Now raise the volume of change tenfold while reading capacity stays flat. The diagnostics
scale with the change. The reading does not. What happens to a safety mechanism whose
output is a queue of things for a human to read, when that queue grows faster than the
human?

It does not fail loudly. It fails by being skipped, batch-approved, or configured to warn
instead of block, which are the same three things that happen to any check that becomes
inconvenient, and which Volume I's Chapter 3 already warned about in its "guardrails, not
gates" discussion: if the platform is worse than routing around it, people route around it,
and you cannot stop them. An agent generating changes faster than review can absorb them is
a very efficient way of making review inconvenient.

**Conclusion: prevention degrades differently, and that difference is the whole point.**

An error that *cannot be expressed* imposes no reading. There is no diagnostic to triage, no
queue to fall behind on, no judgement call to make under time pressure. If the language
cannot represent a selector that disagrees with its labels (because there is only one value
and no syntax for a second), then that class of error is absent from generated
configuration for the same reason it is absent from hand-written configuration. The
generator cannot produce it, not because it was well-behaved, but because the target
language has no way to say it.

That is the asymmetry the argument turns on. **Detection scales with reviewers. Prevention
scales with the generator.**

Volume I's Chapter 3 made this distinction as a matter of cognitive load: a guardrail costs
the developer a little attention, and a mistake that cannot be represented costs nothing.
Volume I's Chapter 14 made it as a matter of economics. My claim is that a third argument
has now arrived, and it is structural rather than aesthetic: when the author is not human,
the mechanism that assumes a human reader is the one that stops working, and the mechanism
that assumes nothing keeps working unchanged.

## The strongest objections, and what I actually think

I would rather state these myself than have them found.

**"Agents can read diagnostics too."** True, and this is the objection I take most seriously.
A well-designed agent loop reads a compiler error and fixes the code. In that world,
detection does not need a human reader.

My answer is partial. It moves the problem rather than removing it: an agent acting on a
diagnostic is making a judgement, and the question becomes whether that judgement is
trustworthy at volume without review, which is the original problem one level up.
Prevention does not have this regress, because there is no judgement to trust. But I concede
that a sufficiently reliable agent-plus-checker loop narrows the gap I am claiming, and that
the size of the remaining gap is an empirical question I cannot settle from an armchair.

**"This is a language-design argument dressed up as an AI argument."** Partly fair. The
prevention-over-detection case does not depend on AI, and Volume I made it without
mentioning AI once. What I am claiming is not that AI created the argument, but that it
changes the *weight*: an argument that was a matter of ergonomics becomes a matter of
whether the safety mechanism functions at all. If you already found the Volume I argument
persuasive, this chapter adds urgency rather than substance.

**"Generated code will simply get good enough."** Maybe. But note that the failure classes
in Volume I are not the ones fluency fixes. A dangling selector is not a knowledge failure;
it is a *coordination* failure between two files, and it is precisely the kind of thing that
is invisible to anything reasoning about one file at a time. I would expect models to get
better at this. I would not expect the category to disappear, because it did not disappear
for humans either, and humans have been fluent in YAML for a decade.

## What would prove me wrong

An argument you cannot falsify is not worth much, so here are the conditions.

**If review capacity scales with generation.** If tooling emerges that reviews
machine-generated infrastructure changes with human-level judgement at machine speed, and it
is trusted enough to be relied on, then premise one fails and the argument collapses. I
would consider this the most likely way I am wrong.

**If the DORA instability finding reverses and stays reversed.** If future reports show
throughput and stability rising together under high AI adoption, the empirical foundation of
premise two is gone. One year's reversal would not settle it; a sustained one would.

**If cross-resource errors stop appearing in generated configuration** at rates comparable
to hand-written configuration, measured rather than asserted. This is the most direct test
and, as far as I know, nobody has run it properly. It is the study I would most like to see,
and the study of real configuration behind Syg measured exactly this class in
human-written configuration, so the comparison is at least methodologically available.

**If prevention turns out to cost more than it saves.** A language that makes errors
unrepresentable also makes some legitimate things unrepresentable: Volume I was explicit
that `minAvailable` is a real choice with a valid range and cannot be designed away. If
that restriction bites harder in practice than the errors it prevents, the trade is bad
regardless of the theory.

## Where this leaves Syg

Briefly, because this volume is not a product pitch.

Syg's design predates this argument. It was built on Volume I's reasoning: that
cross-resource disagreement is a real, measured class of failure, and that deriving both
ends of a relationship from one value makes that class unrepresentable rather than merely
detectable.

What I am claiming here is that the same design answers a question it was not built for. A
compiler that refuses to represent a broken relationship does not care who is typing.
Its guarantee is a property of the language, not of the author's diligence, and that is
exactly the property you want when you no longer know who the author is.

That is a modest claim and I want to keep it modest. Syg addresses one class of error. It
does not make generated infrastructure safe; it makes one specific family of mistakes
absent from it. Volume I's final chapter made a point of preferring many small honest claims
to one large one, and this is one of the small ones.
